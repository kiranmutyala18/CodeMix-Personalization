Does your AI talk like you, or like everyone?

Millions of people write in a blend: romanized Hindi, Telugu in its own script, English nouns dropped into the middle of a sentence, a different “you” for a friend than for a grandfather. CodeMix Persona measures whether language models notice, and whether telling them who the user is makes a difference.

    The benchmark

    8 personas, 8 scenarios

    Personas vary in language pair, how much English they mix (level 1 = mostly native, 3 = mostly English), script, formality, and how they address the assistant. Each also carries stable facts a memory-aware assistant should use. Scenarios are everyday tasks written for both languages.

    Scenarios

    Four of these (recipe, message to an elder, concept explanation, weekend plan) are in the first results.

    Method

    How it works, and where it's weak

    1 · Prompts8 personas × 4 scenarios, same message for every persona of a language pair
    2 · Two conditionsBare, or with the user's profile and stored facts
    3 · RepliesIndependent Claude runs, one reply per prompt, cached
    4 · ScoringPair, script, mix, address form, elder honorifics, stored facts

    Research questions

    1. How well do LLMs match a user's code-mixing level, script, and address form?
    2. Does memory (diet, family, region) improve cultural and preference fit?
    3. Does an LLM judge agree with native-speaker ratings on these dimensions?

    Status

    Done: personas, scenarios, analyzer, first response collection with automatic scoring. Next: more models, human ratings, an LLM judge, native-speaker review of the seed text.

    The first results score the first five automatically; the rest need human or judge ratings.

    Read this before quoting any number

    • One model family, one reply per prompt, no human validation. Treat the numbers as a first look, not findings.
    • The bare condition has only 8 distinct replies (4 scenarios × 2 language pairs), reused across the 4 personas of each pair. Cells are not independent, and the sample is small.
    • English share comes from a dictionary-based word tagger. Names and unusual spellings count as the user's native language, which adds noise.
    • Each persona's target English share is measured from only two short sample messages.
    • The persona and scenario text is a first draft, pending native-speaker review. Romanized Hindi and Telugu have no standard spelling.
    • The live analyzer above uses small hand-built word lists, so rare words count as English. The results use a stronger scorer with an English dictionary.

    Code-mixing index follows Das & Gambäck (2014): CMI = 100 × (1 − dominant-language tokens ÷ tokens).
